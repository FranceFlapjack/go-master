## The game

Shusaku was seventeen and a 4 dan; Gennan Inseki was 8 dan and the strongest player of the Inoue house. Black in those days paid nothing for moving first — no komi — and the game was played over three days.

Step through it with the arrow keys. The comments mark the corners, the enclosures, the approach, the diagonal that took Shusaku's name, and the move the game is remembered for.

## What to look for

**Moves 1–10, the corners.** Three 3-4 points, one approach, one more corner, then two enclosures. Everything the opening track taught, in the order it taught it: corners, then approaches and enclosures, then the sides.

**Move 9, Shusaku's diagonal.** Q15, the quiet answer to White's approach at P17 — the move the joseki track calls Shusaku's diagonal, played here by Shusaku himself.

**Moves 11–25, the taisha.** One of the oldest and most complicated joseki in the game, and outside what this course teaches; watch it as a fight rather than as a sequence to learn.

**Move 127, K11.** The ear-reddening move. A doctor watching is said to have seen Gennan's ears redden when it was played — the story the game is named for. What the move does is simple enough to see on the board: it sits in the middle of everything, helping three different black groups at once and taking the centre away from White.

**Moves 130–325.** The rest is a long endgame with a ko in the middle of the board that is retaken a dozen times. Black wins by two points.

## Remember

- The opening of an 1846 game is the opening this course teaches: corners, approaches, enclosures, sides.
- Shusaku's diagonal was Shusaku's.
- A move can be great for a dull reason: K11 works because it does three ordinary things at once.
